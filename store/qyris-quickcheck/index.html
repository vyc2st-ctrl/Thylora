THE INSTRUMENT

Five passes over one decision

Most bad decisions are not made by people who thought too little. They are made by people who thought about the same part five times and never touched the other four. QYRIS splits one decision into five passes so that each pass has somewhere different to go.

WHAT EACH PASS IS FOR

1

Question

Name the decision you are actually making — the one with a yes, a no, or a choice between named options. Not the topic. Not the worry. The decision.

2

Yield

Name the outcome you are trying to get. What is different afterward if this goes the way you want? Yield is the result, stated plainly enough to check later.

3

Reason

Say why that outcome matters. This is the pass that catches decisions made for a reason you would not defend out loud.

4

Inspect

Separate what you know from what you are inferring, and name what you do not know. Most reversals live here, in the gap between evidence and assumption.

5

Safeguard

Name what must not happen, who else is affected, what needs permission, and what evidence would make you stop. Then you are allowed to move.

HOW LONG THIS TAKES

A small decision runs in about ten minutes. A large one takes an evening and a second pass in the morning. If you find yourself on plate 5 for an hour, that is not lost time — that is the decision telling you it was never simple.

PASS ONE

Question

What decision are you actually making?

Write it as something that can be answered. “My car situation” cannot be answered. “Do I pay for this repair, or put that money toward replacing the car?” can be answered. If your sentence has no verb you could act on this week, you are still holding a topic, not a decision.

PLAIN

What am I deciding? Say it in one sentence, out loud.

EVERYDAY

Write the decision as a question a friend could answer yes or no to, or as a short list of the real options in front of you.

TECHNICAL

State the decision as a bounded choice: the option set, the decision owner, and the point after which the choice is expensive to reverse.

The decision, in one sentence Start with “Do I…”, “Should we…”, or “Which of…”
The real options Two or three. “Do nothing” counts and is often one of them.
Who decides Name the person. If it is shared, say who else has to agree.
When this has to be decided, and what makes it hard to undo after that

A TEST FOR THIS PLATE

Read your sentence to someone who knows nothing about the situation. If their first response is a question rather than an opinion, the decision is not named yet. Rewrite it and read it again.

PASS TWO & THREE

Yield & Reason

What outcome are you trying to get, and why does that outcome matter?

These are two passes on one plate because they check each other. A yield you cannot state is a wish. A reason you would not say out loud is usually the real reason, and it deserves to be written down where you can look at it.

Yield is not the action“Get the car fixed” is the action. “Reliable transport to work for the next year without a new monthly payment” is the yield.
Yield is checkableWrite it so that in three months you could say plainly whether you got it.
Reason is allowed to be personal“Because I am tired of worrying about it every morning” is a legitimate reason. Write the true one.
One yield at a timeIf you have three, you have three decisions. Put two of them on a separate copy.
Yield — the outcome you are trying to get What is actually different afterward?
How you would know, later, that you got it
Reason — why that outcome matters The true reason, not the presentable one.
What you are willing to give up to get it Money, time, convenience, something else. Name it now, before you are mid-decision.

WHEN THESE TWO DISAGREE

If your reason does not actually point at your yield — if the reason is “I want to stop worrying” but the yield is “save money” — stop here. One of the two is borrowed from someone else’s version of this decision. Fix it before you inspect anything, or you will inspect the wrong facts.

PASS FOUR

Inspect

Five columns. Most of what you “know” belongs in two of them.

Known Facts you could show someone today.
Evidence For each known: what proves it? A quote, a statement, a document, a reading, something you measured.
Inference What you are concluding from those facts. Write it as “I think… because…”
Unknown What you genuinely cannot say yet — and which of these you could find out before deciding.
Connections What else this decision touches: other people, other money, other commitments, later dates.

THE RULE THAT DOES THE WORK

An unknown is not a false. It is not a yes either. A thing you have not checked stays in the unknown column until you check it, and a decision that only works if an unknown turns out your way is a decision waiting on a fact you have not got.

PASS FIVE

Safeguard

The last pass, and the only one with the authority to stop you.

Safeguard is not pessimism. It is the pass where you decide, in advance and while calm, what would count as a reason to stop — so that the decision to stop is not made later, under pressure, by whoever is most tired.

What must not happen The outcome that would make this decision wrong no matter what else went right.
Who else is affected Name them. Household, work, anyone relying on the thing you are deciding about.
What needs permission — and whose Money that is not only yours, a shared calendar, a lease, a contract, a manager, a co-owner.
What evidence would make you stop Write it as a specific finding, not a feeling. “If the second quote is above X.”

BEFORE YOU MOVE — CHECK EACH ONE HONESTLY

THREE ENDINGS, NOT ONE

Act — the passes hold. Move, and keep your stop-condition where you can see it.

Ask — a permission or a person is missing. Go get that, then return to this plate.

Hold — an unknown would change the answer. Name the one fact that would release it, and go find that fact. Holding on purpose is a decision; drifting is not.

WORKED EXAMPLE

A repair quote, and a car worth about three of them

An ordinary decision, run all the way through, so you can see what a finished QuickCheck actually looks like.

The situation

A twelve-year-old car needs a transmission repair. The shop quotes $1,400. The car is worth roughly $3,000 as it stands. It is driven about nine miles each way to work, five days a week. There is $2,600 in savings. No car payment at present.

QUESTION

Do I pay $1,400 for this repair, or stop repairing this car and put the money toward replacing it within the next two months?

Options: repair now · start replacing now · do nothing and keep driving it as-is. Decides: me, but the household calendar depends on the car, so one other person has to agree to the timing.

YIELD

Reliable transport to work for at least the next twelve months, without taking on a monthly payment.

How I would know: twelve months from now, I have not missed work over the car and I have no new loan.

REASON

Missing shifts costs me more than the repair does, and the uncertainty is the part I actually hate — I have been checking the noise every morning for three weeks. That is the true reason, and it is about steadiness, not about the car.

Willing to give up: most of the savings cushion, but not all of it.

INSPECT

KnownQuote is $1,400, written. Car is worth about $3,000. Savings are $2,600. Commute is 18 miles round trip. No current payment.
EvidenceThe written quote from the shop. Two online valuations for the same year, model and mileage. My bank balance. The odometer.
InferenceI think this repair buys another year or two, because the rest of the car has been maintained. That is a conclusion, not a fact — the shop did not promise a year.
UnknownWhether anything else is close to failing. What a comparable replacement actually costs this month. Whether a second shop quotes lower. All three are findable this week.
ConnectionsOne other person’s schedule depends on this car. Spending $1,400 leaves $1,200 of cushion. A replacement search would take evenings I do not currently have free.

SAFEGUARD

Must not happenPaying $1,400 and then having the car fail again within a few months, with the cushion already spent.
Who else is affectedThe person whose schedule depends on this car. They need to know the timing either way.
Needs permissionThe savings are shared. Spending more than half of them is a conversation, not a solo decision.
Would make me stopIf an inspection finds a second failure that is also expensive, or if a second quote comes back materially higher than the first.

WHAT THE INSTRUMENT ACTUALLY RETURNED

Hold — for six days, on purpose. Not because the repair is wrong. Because three unknowns are all findable inside a week, and every one of them could change the answer.

The releasing facts, named: a second quote, an inspection for anything else close to failing, and one real look at replacement prices this month.

Notice what happened. Nothing in the situation changed. The decision was still hard. But the question stopped being “repair or replace?” — which had no answer — and became “get three facts by Friday”, which does. That is the entire value of the five passes.
