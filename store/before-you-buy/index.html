A claim is not evidence

Almost every bad purchase is made by someone who was told the truth. The seller said what the thing does; the buyer heard what they needed it to do; nobody lied. This packet exists to put a column between those two things.

ThisIs not this
Claim — what is said about itEvidence — what you have seen with your own eyes, or what a person who has used it told you directly
Price — the number on the pageTotal cost — price, plus setup, plus the hours, plus the thing you must keep paying for, plus what it costs to leave
Feature — what it can doConstraint — what it will not do, what it requires, and who it locks you to
Confidence — how sure the seller soundsUnknown — what neither of you can answer yet, written down where it cannot be forgotten

The seven columns

  1. Claim — one claim per line, in their words, not yours.
  2. Source — who said it: the seller, their website, a review, a person you know, you.
  3. Evidence — what you have actually seen. Most lines will be empty at first. That is the finding.
  4. Total cost — everything the claim commits you to, not just the sticker.
  5. Constraints — what it needs, refuses, or ties you to.
  6. Unknown — the honest blanks, named.
  7. Act · Ask · Hold — the verdict for that line, before the verdict for the whole thing.
Act Every claim that costs money has evidence behind it, and the total cost is one you can carry if it disappoints.
Ask One question, to a named person, would turn an unknown into evidence. Ask it. Most purchases stop here for a day.
Hold A claim you are relying on has no evidence, the exit cost is high, or the unknown is the thing you are buying it for.

Hold is not no. Hold is "not on this information" — and it names what would change it.

01

Claim

Write each claim separately, in their words. "Saves you a day a week" is one claim. "Works with your system" is another. Do not merge them — merged claims cannot be checked.

Watch for claims with no subject: "faster", "cheaper", "easier". Faster than what, measured how?

02

Source

Name who the claim came from. Seller · their website · a review · someone you know · an assumption of your own that has quietly become a fact.

RouteMark your own assumptions honestly. They are the hardest claims to check, because nobody will ever contradict them.
03

Evidence

What have you actually seen? A demonstration on your own data. A trial you ran. A named person who uses it telling you directly. A written guarantee.

Not evidence a case study on their site, a testimonial with a first name only, an award, a number with no method, or how long the call lasted.

RouteLeave the cell empty rather than filling it with something that is really a claim. Empty cells are the point of this page.
04

Total cost

The price is the first line, not the answer. Add up all six:

PriceThe number on the page, for the term you will actually use it.
SetupInstallation, migration, deposit, delivery, training, the weekend somebody loses.
Your hoursHours × what an hour of your time is worth. Usually the largest line, and the one nobody writes down.
CarryWhat keeps being paid: licences, maintenance, storage, insurance, the person who has to keep it running.
ExitWhat it costs to stop — notice period, getting your data or stock out, retraining, the contract you signed.
If it disappointsWhat you are out if it simply does not work. If that number would hurt, the verdict is not Act.

05

Constraints

What does it require, refuse, or tie you to?

  • Requires — a thing you must already have, or must also buy.
  • Refuses — what it will not do, said plainly. Every product refuses something; a seller who names it is worth more than one who does not.
  • Ties — minimum term, exclusivity, who holds your data, who holds your stock, and how hard it is to take it back.
RouteAsk directly: "What does this not do that people expect it to?" An answer of "nothing" is itself a finding.
06

Unknown

The things neither of you can answer yet. Write them down; an unwritten unknown becomes an assumption within a day.

Mark each one as fatal (you cannot decide without it), costly (it changes the number), or survivable (you can find out afterwards).

RouteA fatal unknown makes the verdict Ask or Hold, whatever the rest of the page says. Never trade a fatal unknown for a discount.
07

Act · Ask · Hold

Per line first, then for the whole purchase. The whole is never better than its worst line that involves money.

Act when every money-bearing claim has evidence and you could carry the disappointment. Ask when one named question would settle it — and it is asked today, not "soon". Hold when the evidence is missing, the exit is expensive, or the unknown is the very thing you are buying.

Three sentences that do most of the work

"Can I see that on my own data, with my own numbers?" · "What does it cost me to stop after three months?" · "Who else your size uses it, and may I speak to them?"
A seller who can answer all three is usually worth buying from. One who cannot answer any of them has told you something useful, for free.

Worked example

Thornbury Grounds, a fictional nine-person landscaping firm, is considering a scheduling and invoicing tool called Brightloom, quoted at 29 a month per user on an annual contract. The owner fills the page before the follow-up call.

ClaimSourceEvidence Verdict
"Saves each crew lead about a day a week." Seller, on the call None. Asked how it was measured: it is an average across all customers, not a figure from a firm of this size or type. Ask
"Works with the invoicing you already use." Their website Partly. Export works; import back does not. Seen in a shared-screen demonstration, on our own file. Act
"Your crews will pick it up in a day." Review site One named contact at a similar firm said two weeks, and that two of six staff still use paper. Spoke to them directly. Ask
"We can cancel any time." Our own assumption Contradicted by the contract: annual term, 60 days' notice, no refund of the unused part. Hold

Total cost — the number that is actually being decided

Price29 × 9 users × 12 months = 3,132 for the year
SetupData migration quoted at 400, plus a half-day of training for nine people
Your hoursOwner: ~20 hours over six weeks. Crew leads: 4 hours each. At 35/hour that is about 1,400
CarrySomebody must own it. Half a day a month, about 210 a year
Exit60 days' notice, no refund, and the schedule history exports as PDF only — it would be retyped
If it disappointsRoughly 5,100 and a disrupted season. That is survivable, but it is not the 3,132 the decision was about.

Every figure above is fictional. Thornbury Grounds and Brightloom do not exist.

Constraints

  • Requires a phone per crew lead with current software — two would need replacing, about 500.
  • Refuses split invoices, which this firm uses on every commercial job. The seller confirmed it, unprompted, which raised confidence in everything else they said.
  • Ties: annual term, 60 days' notice, history exports as PDF only.

Unknown

  • Fatal — whether split invoicing can be worked around without typing every commercial job twice.
  • Costly — whether the day-a-week saving holds for a crew of nine rather than the average customer.
  • Survivable — whether the two paper-preferring staff will adopt it.

The verdict

Act No. A fatal unknown sits directly on the work this firm charges for.
Ask Taken. Two questions, in writing, by Friday: can split invoices be produced at all, and will they run a 30-day trial on our real jobs before the annual term starts?
Hold Held in reserve: if the answer to split invoicing is no, this becomes a Hold and the notes say exactly why.

What the page was worth

Fifteen minutes turned a 3,132 decision into a 5,100 one — the same purchase, honestly counted. It found a cancellation assumption that the contract contradicts, replaced an average with a figure from a comparable firm, and surfaced one refusal that decides the whole thing.

It also left a written record. If the answer comes back yes and the tool is bought, this page is what the firm reads in a year when somebody asks why.

Provenance

The claim-versus-evidence column comes from a rule proved inside THYLORA production, where the word "paid" was made to require a date, a processor, a reference and a matching amount before any system would accept it. The total-cost ladder comes from the same discipline applied to money that has not moved yet: a modelled figure is labelled as modelled, never quoted as a fact.

Nothing in the worked example is real. Thornbury Grounds and Brightloom do not exist, and no price, contract or person in it refers to anyone.
