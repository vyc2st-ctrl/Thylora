THE CLERK AND THE JUDGE
Building a working relationship that holds up for everyone who depends on the record

1. WHY THIS RELATIONSHIP MATTERS

Every person in a courtroom depends on the record the clerk and the judge make together.
The parties, the attorneys, the jail, probation, the next judge, the appeals court.
When the record is accurate, consistent and concise, nobody has to come back and ask.
When there is a fault, every person downstream waits.
A ten-second gap at the bench can become a two-week delay for a family.

The clerk and the judge are not boss and helper.
They are two halves of one record.

2. THE FIRST WEEK WITH A NEW JUDGE

A new judge does not yet know how this courtroom runs.
The clerk does.
The first week sets the pattern for years.

Day 1: Exchange intake sheets (section 3). Do not skip this for a hallway hello.
Day 1: Agree on how the judge will signal a ruling is final, and how the clerk will signal she needs it repeated.
Day 2: Walk through the docket together before court, not during.
Day 3: After court, spend ten minutes on what slowed the room down today.
Day 5: Read back one full day's record together. Mark anything that could be read two ways.
End of week: Each fills in the Preference Card (section 4) from what they learned.

3. THE TWO-WAY INTAKE: YOU FILL OUT MINE, I FILL OUT YOURS

The judge answers the clerk's questions. The clerk answers the judge's questions.
Then they sit down, not for five minutes, and go over both.

Questions the clerk asks the judge:
- How do you want to be told when a party or attorney is not present?
- When you say "recess," do you want me to announce a clock time?
- Do you read dispositions aloud in full, or do you hand me the form?
- What should I never interrupt you for? What should I always interrupt you for?
- How do you want corrections raised: on the record, at sidebar, or after court?

Questions the judge asks the clerk:
- What do new judges usually do that slows you down?
- What do you need from me before court starts?
- Where in a hearing do you most often have to guess?
- What would make your entries faster without making them less accurate?
- How do I tell you I made a mistake on the record?

Both sign the bottom.
Signing means: I understood this, and I will keep learning past it.

5. THE FIVE HABITS THAT KEEP THE RECORD CLEAN

One. Clock times, not durations. "Return at 10:40 AM," not "back in ten."
Two. Who tells whom. Every update names who gave it and who received it.
Three. The reason in plain words. "To review Exhibit 4," not "per bench."
Four. Same words for the same thing, every day. If it is "continued" on Monday, it is not "reset" on Tuesday.
Five. Read back before you close. If one line can be read two ways, fix it before it leaves the room.

6. WHEN SOMETHING GOES WRONG

Fault found during court: raise it at the agreed signal. Fix it on the record.
Fault found after court: tell the judge the same day, in the way the card says.
Fault found by someone downstream: own it, correct it, and add it to the ten-minute review so it does not repeat.

The rule: a fault is not a failure. Hiding a fault is.

7. ONE WAY TO MEASURE IT

Record value = Accurate x Timely x Usable x Relied on

Each part is scored from 0 to 1.
Multiply them. Do not add them.
If any one part is 0, the record is worth 0.
A record that is perfect but late is not mostly good. To the person waiting on it, it is not there yet.

Formula name: Practical Value of a record (PV = A x T x U x R), from Open Door Packet v4.

8. WHAT THE RELATIONSHIP LOOKS LIKE AFTER 30 DAYS

The judge does not have to repeat rulings.
The clerk does not have to guess.
Recesses end at the time announced.
Attorneys stop asking the clerk what the judge meant.
The next judge who takes this courtroom inherits a card, not a mystery.
