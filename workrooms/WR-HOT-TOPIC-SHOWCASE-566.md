## D · THE POST — EXACT CAPTION

**Your assistant can book the flight now. Who decided it was allowed to?**

This month Meta launched Muse, a personal agent that connects to email, calendar, payments,
health, shopping and smart home, and works through multi-step tasks with little supervision.
Deloitte is already describing the next step: your AI talking to a store's AI, no webpage
involved.

**What changed:** assistants stopped suggesting and started doing. Recommending a flight and
buying one are not the same act, and the second one spends your money.

**The question most people are not asking:** everybody is arguing about what these agents can
*reach*. Almost nobody is writing down what they are *allowed to decide*.

**ACCESS ≠ AUTHORITY.**

Access is the door. Authority is the decision. Your assistant having your inbox is access.
Your assistant deciding which email gets answered as you is authority. Those are two different
grants, and most products only ask you for the first one.

Six questions worth asking before you hand over anything:

1. **IDENTITY** — when it acts, whose name is on it? Yours, or the company's?
2. **ACCESS** — what can it reach today? Not what it uses. What it *can* reach.
3. **AUTHORITY** — what can it decide without asking you? Name the dollar limit out loud.
4. **EVIDENCE** — after it acts, can you see what it acted on? Or only what it did?
5. **MEMORY** — what does it keep, for how long, and can you make it forget?
6. **PROVENANCE** — when it tells you something, can it show you where that came from?

If a product can't answer all six, it has your access without your authority.

**Why this is not theoretical.** Security researchers spent this year documenting *indirect
prompt injection* — instructions hidden in a webpage or a document that the agent reads and
obeys as if they were yours. In March a widely used AI package was backdoored on PyPI after
attackers got publishing credentials through a compromised security scanner in the build
pipeline. Same shape every time: the agent had access, someone else supplied the instruction,
and nothing in between asked whether it had the authority.

**Your turn.** If your assistant could act for you tomorrow — what's the one thing it should
never do without asking you first? Mine's money. Say yours.

Follow for the rest of this series. Save this one for the next time an app asks you to
"connect your accounts."

(2026-05-07); Datadog Security Labs, Snyk, Cycode and the LiteLLM advisory (2026-03-24).
Reported figures as published; not independently verified by us.*

## K · BACKUP PACKET — COPY ONLY, NOT PUBLISHED

### DEAD SEA SCROLLS · THE 364-DAY QUMRAN CALENDAR
### Angle: **"SOLVED" is not the same as "FOUND."**

No image generation. No publication. Copy only. Lane-firewalled from the AI-agent packet in the
graph (`SEPARATE_FROM`), so neither packet may borrow the other's evidence, imagery or CTA.

**What the scrolls document.** The Qumran calendrical texts describe a year of exactly 364 days,
divided into four quarters, with the priestly courses (*mishmarot*) rotating on a fixed cycle.
The scrolls are physical objects; the calendar is written in them. That part is not in dispute.

**What older scholarship established.** 364 is the design, not an error. It is divisible by 7,
so the year is exactly 52 weeks and every festival falls on the same weekday, every year,
forever. That is the whole point of it — a calendar no festival can drift across. Scholars have
argued for decades about whether a community ever actually *lived* by it, because a 364-day year
cannot hold its place against the sun.

**What the new study proposes.** *(SOURCE CLAIM — search-result reporting from ScienceDaily
2026-09-19, JNS, Ancient Origins, SciTechDaily and Biblical Archaeology Society; the study is
associated with Prof. Eshbal Ratzon and Tel Aviv University. No source page was read directly
from this session.)* That both answers are true at different times: the community used the
calendar for a period, then abandoned it as seasonal drift accumulated and relations with the
Hasmonean rulers changed under Alexander Jannaeus — after which it survived as a religious ideal
and a marker of identity rather than a working schedule.

**The simple drift math.** *(THYLORA ANALYSIS — arithmetic, certain.)*

- 364 ÷ 7 = **52 weeks exactly.** Fixed weekdays, permanently. That is what it buys.
- Tropical year = 365.2422 days. 365.2422 − 364 = **1.2422 days lost every year.** That is what it costs.

| Elapsed | Drift |
|---|---|
| 3 years | 3.7 days |
| 10 years | 12.4 days |
| 25 years | 31.1 days — about a month |
| 33 years | 41.0 days |
| 100 years | 124.2 days — a third of a year |

Against a 365-day civil year it loses exactly 1 day per year. A spring festival stays a spring
festival for a generation. It does not stay one for a century. Something has to give, and the
study's proposal is that what gave was the practice, not the belief.

**What remains unknown.**
- Whether any scroll records an *observed* intercalation — an actual correction being made.
- The date the community stopped using it.
- Whether it was ever the *only* calendar in use, rather than one of several running in parallel.
- Whether the 2026 study says what the secondary reporting says it says — no primary source or
  journal article was read from this session.

**Why the angle holds.** A 2,000-year-old object was *found*. A modern argument about how people
used it has been *proposed*. Headlines merge those two things. The scroll is evidence. The
reading of the scroll is a claim. Knowing which one you are holding is the whole discipline.

Recorded as `FIND-QUMRAN-364-DRIFT-566`.
