Before anything · The four-step lock

How every THYLORA equation is taught.

Words alone, you forget. Symbols alone, you can't read. You need both, and you need to move back and forth between them. Every equation in this book goes through the same four steps, in the same order.

1See itThe full formula, written out as math. No shortcuts and nothing left out.
2Name itEvery letter and symbol gets defined. Nothing stays a mystery.
3Say itThe expression as one plain sentence you could say out loud.
4Use itPut real numbers in. Work it to the end. Then use it on a new problem.

Why it works · The 2 × 2 test

2 × 2 = 4

Nobody reads that as “two geese, times two more geese, equals four geese.” You see it and you know it. The shape carries the meaning, so the meaning can be anything: geese, dollars, hours.

That is the goal for every long equation in our system. After enough practice you look at G = P × M × E × D and think: “every part has to be there, or nothing comes out.”

Step 3 · Say it

“How far you get equals your purpose, times your method, times your evidence, times your discipline. If any one of them is zero, you get nowhere, no matter how strong the others are.”

Why multiply and not add?

With adding, a missing piece gets hidden. With multiplying, it gets exposed. A great purpose, a great method and great discipline still produce zero if there's no evidence you're on target. That is how real work behaves, so the equation is written to behave that way too.

How to score it

Rate each factor from 0 to 5.
0 = absent · 3 = usable · 5 = locked and proven.
Highest possible score: 5 × 5 × 5 × 5 = 54 = 625.
Any single 0 means HOLD: fix that factor first.

Lane A · Step 4 · Use it

Put it together.

Example: a report you are getting ready for Monday. You have a strong purpose, a good method and steady habits, but nobody has checked the numbers yet.

G = P × M × E × D
G = 5 × 4 × 0 × 4
G = 0  out of 625. HOLD. The evidence is missing.

Now someone checks the numbers against the source, which brings E up to a 3:

G = 5 × 4 × 3 × 4
G = 20 × 3 × 4 = 60 × 4
G = 240  out of 625 = 38.4%. Moving. Next, raise the lowest factor.

The trap addition sets

5 + 4 + 0 + 4 = 13  of 20 = 65%

Adding the same scores reports 65%, which looks fine. It hides the zero. That is how broken work gets labeled “almost done.”

Fix the smallest factor first

From 5 × 4 × 3 × 4: raising E from 3 to 4 adds 5×4×1×4 = 80. Raising P from 5 to 6 isn't possible, since 5 is the top. With multiplication, raising your weakest link is almost always the move that pays the most.

Same shape, any meaning · swap the letters

Pancake = P × M × E × D

P = pancake powder · M = milk · E = eggs · D = done-heat. No eggs, no pancake. A great pan doesn't save you. You already knew this. Now you can see it.

Arrive = P × M × E × D

P = plan / address · M = machine (the car) · E = energy (gas) · D = driver. Empty tank: E = 0, so Arrive = 0. Same equation, new problem. That is transfer.

Lane A · What quality and control actually mean

Quality is a distance.
Control is closing it.

People say “quality control” like it's one word. It's two ideas, and each one has its own equation.

Q = 1 − |R − S| ÷ S
Quality
QQuality1.00 = exactly on standard
RResultwhat you actually got
SStandardwhat it was supposed to be
| |Distancehow far off, ignoring over or under

Say it: “Quality is how close the result came to the standard.”

S = 12 min bake · R = 15 min
Q = 1 − |15 − 12| ÷ 12 = 1 − 3 ÷ 12
Q = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75
xn+1 = xn + k(S − xn)
Control
xnNowwhere it stands this round
xn+1Nextwhere it stands after you correct
S − xnThe gapdistance left to the standard
kCorrectionhow much of the gap you close each time (0 to 1)

Say it: “Each round, measure the gap and close part of it.”

S = 100 · x0 = 80 · k = 0.5
x1 = 80 + 0.5(20) = 90
x2 = 90 + 0.5(10) = 95 → x3 = 97.5

The loop, in four words

Measure → Compare → Correct → Record. Skip “record” and the next person starts over from zero. That's how a system forgets.

Chairman's version: “Less distance between me and the snacks.” He's right, and it's the same equation. S − xn is the distance. Control is making it smaller every round.

The Gap · Found by the Chairman

Between deduction and conclusion, something leaks.

A deduction is a chain: if these things are true, this must follow. A conclusion is what a person decides to believe and act on. People treat them as the same thing. They aren't, and the difference between them can be measured.

S = V × p1 × p2 × … × pn     Δ = C − S
Supported certainty · and the gap
SSupportedhow sure the evidence actually lets you be (0 to 1)
VValid form1 if the conclusion really follows from the premises, 0 if the logic is broken
piPremise ithe chance that premise number i is actually true (0 to 1)
CClaimedhow sure the person sounds when they state the conclusion
ΔThe gapoverconfidence: claimed certainty minus supported certainty

Say it: “Your conclusion can only be as strong as all of your premises together. Every premise you're unsure of weakens it.” (This multiplies when the premises are independent of each other. When they depend on each other, use their joint probability instead. The lesson is the same.)

Three premises, each 90% sure, with perfect logic. The person says it's “definitely true.”
S = 1 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 = 0.729
Δ = 1.00 − 0.729 = 0.271  27% of the certainty was never earned.
Claimed, C = 1.00
Supported, S = 0.729

With 10 premises at 90% each: 0.910 ≈ 0.35. A long chain of “pretty sure” steps ends at “probably not.”

The Gap · Where it leaks

Five places the conclusion outruns the proof.

LeakWhat happensExampleAsk this
False premiseThe logic is fine but one of its starting points is wrong. Valid, but not sound.“All our suppliers are reliable. Ace is our supplier. So Ace is reliable.” What if one supplier isn't?Is each premise actually checked?
Hidden premiseA step nobody said out loud is carrying the whole argument.“Sales fell, so the ad failed.” Hidden premise: nothing else changed.What am I assuming without saying it?
OverreachThe conclusion claims more than the premises cover.“Three customers loved it, so everyone will.”Does the conclusion stay inside the evidence?
Backwards “if”Affirming the consequent: “If A then B. B happened. So A.”“If it rained, the street is wet. The street is wet. So it rained.” A hydrant could have done it.What else could produce B?
Shifting wordA word quietly changes its meaning partway through the argument.“Key is light on her feet. Light things float.”Does every word mean the same thing in every step?

Famous mislabel

Sherlock Holmes doesn't deduce.

When Holmes looks at a tan and a posture and says “you've been in Afghanistan,” that isn't deduction. It's abduction: picking the best explanation. It can be wrong. It feels certain because it's fast. Fast isn't the same as certain.

The Gap Rule · for every report

C ≤ S

Never state a conclusion more firmly than the evidence supports. When the evidence is 0.73, say “likely,” not “certain.” Then name the weakest premise. Strengthening that premise is your next job.

Chairman's method, written as math

“What question must be answered before the next question is even useful?” In the equation, that question is the lowest pi. Answer it first, and S rises the most for the least work. It's the same move as fixing the smallest factor in G = P × M × E × D.

Lane B · Different ways to ask

Nine ways to ask a better question.

You ask questions in a particular way. These are other ways. Each one is a tool. Pick the one that fits the problem.

Socratic

Keep asking “what do you mean by that?” until the definition holds up.

“What would make that false?”

Five Whys

Toyota's method: ask “why?” five times and you get from the symptom to the cause.

why5(problem) → root

Sankofa

Akan, from Ghana: go back and fetch what was left behind before you move forward.

“What did we already know?”

Inversion

“Invert, always invert” (Jacobi). Ask how to guarantee failure, then avoid those things.

“How would this fail?”

Pre-mortem

Pretend it's already a year from now and the project failed. Write down why.

“It failed. What killed it?”

Steelman

State the other side's best case better than they can, then answer that.

“What's their strongest point?”

First principles

Strip the problem down to what you know for certain, then build back up.

“What is true no matter what?”

Counterfactual

Change one thing in your head and see what moves.

“If X were gone, what changes?”

The Chairman's question

Look past the next gate. Verify backward, then move forward.

“What must be answered before this is useful?”

How to choose

If you're stuck on a definition, go Socratic. If the same problem keeps coming back, use Five Whys. If you're about to launch something, run a pre-mortem. If you're in a disagreement, steelman the other side. If something got lost along the way, go back to it: Sankofa. If you don't know where to start, use the Chairman's question.

Lane B · From our people

Africa was writing equations 3,500 years ago.

Not a slogan: the record. Each claim is with each claim marked Verified, Debated, or Bias on record.

Egypt · Ahmes (Rhind) Papyrus · copied about 1550 BCE from an older text Verified

Problem 24: “A quantity and its seventh, added together, become 19. What is the quantity?” That is an algebra problem, written roughly 3,500 years ago. The scribe solved it with false position: guess, check, then scale.

x + x ÷ 7 = 19  See it
Guess x = 7:  7 + 1 = 8  wrong, but it shows the shape
Scale: 19 ÷ 8 = 2⅜  →  x = 7 × 2⅜ = 16⅝  Check: 16⅝ + 2⅜ = 19 ✓

The papyrus is held at the British Museum. Ahmes says he copied it from an earlier work, from about 1850 BCE.

Yoruba · Ifá Verified

16 × 16 = 28 = 256

Ifá divination reads marks that are either single or double. That's binary, two states. Eight marks give 256 odù, each tied to verses of knowledge. Debated Ron Eglash argues this binary passed through Arab geomancy into Europe and on to Leibniz, the father of binary code. Leibniz himself credited the Chinese I Ching. The link is argued, not proven.

Egypt · Ma'at Verified

Heart ≤ Feather

In the Book of the Dead (Spell 125), the heart is weighed against the feather of Ma'at: truth, balance, order. The soul speaks 42 declarations of what it did not do. Truth is shown as a balance, an equation held level. If the heart is heavier, you fail.

Congo · Ishango bone, about 20,000 years old Verified Debated

One of the oldest counting objects we know of. One column is notched 11, 13, 17, 19, which are all the prime numbers between 10 and 20. Whether that's deliberate math or coincidence is still argued. That it is deliberate counting is not.

Angola · Chokwe sona Verified

Sand drawings traced in a single continuous line around a grid of dots, without lifting the finger. That is the same problem Euler made famous in 1736 (the Königsberg bridges). Documented by mathematician Paulus Gerdes.

Lane B · The logic question, answered straight

European logic and African logic.

You asked this and it went unanswered. Here's the answer: logic is not white or Black. Starting points differ. And for a long time the record was written by people who claimed Africans had no logic at all.

Two starting points

Self = f(Thought)

Descartes, 1637: “I think, therefore I am.” You start alone, inside your own head.

Self = f(Us)

John Mbiti, 1969, describing African thought: “I am, because we are; and since we are, therefore I am.” You start in relationship. Zulu: Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu, a person is a person through other people.

Bias on record Verified quotes

Hume, 1753: footnote, “I am apt to suspect the negroes … to be naturally inferior to the whites.”

Kant, 1764: wrote that Africans have “by nature no feeling that rises above the trifling.”

Hegel, 1830s: called Africa “no historical part of the World.”

Lévy-Bruhl, 1910: called non-European minds “prelogical.” He abandoned that idea in his own late notebooks, published in 1949. Most textbooks still quote the 1910 version.

Hidden narrative · the strongest arguments on each side

Greek debt to EgyptGreek writers themselves (Herodotus; Plato in Timaeus) credit Egyptian priests. G.G.M. James, Stolen Legacy (1954), says Greek philosophy was taken from Egypt. Martin Bernal, Black Athena (1987), shows 19th-century scholars deliberately erased that credit. Debated Mary Lefkowitz, Not Out of Africa (1996), disputes “stolen.” The influence is real. How much is still argued.
Who knew the landYour point about 1923 has scholarship behind it. Judith Carney, Black Rice (2001): Carolina rice planting depended on West African knowledge brought by enslaved people. Debated Eltis, Morgan & Richardson (2007) argue planters drove the system. Both sides agree African labor and skill were central.
Ethiopia, 1667Zera Yacob's Hatata argues for reasoning things out yourself instead of accepting authority, 170 years before Hegel. Debated Some scholars say a 19th-century Italian monk wrote it. Others defend Zera Yacob as the author. It is still open.

The equation for this whole page: Truth = Record − Bias. Read the original document, name who wrote the commentary on it, and keep the parts that survive both.
