## The record

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Land** | The Nile Valley — Kemet, the land its own people called the Black Land, for the silt the flood left behind |
| **People** | Egyptians of the New Kingdom |
| **Period of practice** | New Kingdom and earlier; the text copies older material |
| **Source** | The **Ebers Papyrus** |
| **Date of source** | c. 1550 BCE |
| **Held at** | Leipzig University Library, Germany |
| **How it reached Europe** | Obtained in Luxor; acquired by the German Egyptologist Georg Ebers in 1873, whose name it now carries — not the name of anyone who wrote it |
| **Scale** | A scroll roughly 20 metres long, carrying on the order of 700 remedies and formulas |

The Ebers Papyrus is one of the oldest substantially surviving medical texts anywhere.
It is not folklore collected later by outsiders. It is a working document, written in
Egyptian, by Egyptians, for Egyptian practitioners — which is precisely why it is the
right place to begin a series about what people used before the drugstore.

**A note on the name.** Every time this document is cited it is called by the name of
the 19th-century European who bought it. The people who compiled it are unnamed. That
is not a neutral fact about filing; it is what the record looks like after it passes
through a collector's hands. The series records it as it is, and says so.

## What the source attributes

### Honey — wound dressing

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Attributed use** | Applied to wounds, in dressings and salves |
| **Confidence** | `ATTESTED` — honey is among the most frequently occurring ingredients across the Egyptian medical papyri, including wound treatment |
| **Toxicity** | Low for adults. **Hazard: infant botulism.** Honey must never be given to a child under one year. This is a modern, well-established finding, not an ancient one — the Egyptians had no way to know it |

**What modern pharmacology did with it.** This one carried forward. Medical-grade honey
is used in contemporary wound care. The mechanism is understood now in a way it could not
have been then: honey draws water out of bacterial cells, is acidic, and some varieties
generate low levels of hydrogen peroxide. The Egyptians observed that treated wounds did
better. They were observing something real.

### Castor oil — from *Ricinus communis*

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Attributed use** | Named in the Egyptian medical material, including as a purgative and in ointments; the castor plant was cultivated in Egypt and its seeds have been recovered from tombs |
| **Confidence** | `ATTESTED` for its presence and medicinal naming in the corpus |
| **Toxicity** | **Serious. Named hazard: ricin.** The castor *seed* contains ricin, one of the most toxic substances of natural origin — a small number of chewed seeds can kill an adult. The pressed and processed *oil* does not contain it, because ricin stays behind in the mash. The difference between the oil and the bean is the difference between a laxative and a lethal poison |

**What modern pharmacology did with it.** Castor oil remains a recognised stimulant
laxative. Its active component, ricinoleic acid, acts on receptors in the gut wall. The
ancient use and the modern use point at the same effect.

And the plant's other half went a very different way. Ricin — the toxin the Egyptians
were, by processing, unknowingly removing — became a studied biological agent in the
20th century. One plant, two histories.

### Garlic and onion

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Attributed use** | Present in the Egyptian medical and dietary material; onions and garlic are also recovered archaeologically from Egyptian burials |
| **Confidence** | `REPORTED` — the plants' presence and importance are firmly attested; a specific attributed indication should be quoted only from a checked translation line, which this sheet does not yet have |
| **Toxicity** | Low as food. Garlic in concentrated form can thin the blood and interacts with anticoagulant medication |

**What modern pharmacology did with it.** Partly. Garlic's allicin has genuine
antibacterial activity in the laboratory. It did not become a frontline antibiotic —
laboratory activity and a working drug are not the same thing, and the gap between them
is where most of these stories actually end.

## What this entry is not

It is not "ancient Egyptian medicine" as a single thing. The Ebers Papyrus sits beside
other Egyptian medical texts — the Edwin Smith Papyrus, for one, which is far more
surgical and far more observational in character. Different documents, different
emphases, sometimes different centuries. **One scroll is one source, not a civilisation's
whole mind.** A later entry on the same land will be a different entry.

It is also not a story about people who were guessing. Some of what is in this scroll is
incantation. Some of it is careful pharmacy. Both are in there, side by side, and reading
only the parts that flatter modern medicine is its own kind of flattening.
