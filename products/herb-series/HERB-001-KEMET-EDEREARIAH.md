# The Scroll That Kept Its Secrets
## An EdereAriah mirror companion to Entry 001 — Nile Valley (Kemet)

**Mirror companion** · THYLORA / ErsatzReality
**Entry code:** `THY-HERB-001-MIRROR` · **Pair:** `THY-HERB-001-ADULT`
**Read with a grown-up.**

> This is a story about people who lived a long time ago. It does not tell you to use
> any plant for anything, and it does not tell you how anything was made. Plants can be
> dangerous, and the people in this story knew that better than most.

---

### Before the drugstore

There is no drugstore in this story. There is no shelf with little boxes on it, no
pharmacy counter, no bottle with a label telling you exactly how much.

There is a river.

The river is the Nile, and every year it floods, and when the water goes back down it
leaves behind a stripe of black soil so rich that things grow in it like they are in a
hurry. The people who lived there named their whole country after that soil. Not after a
king. After the dirt that fed them. They called it **Kemet** — the Black Land.

And in the Black Land, about three and a half thousand years ago, somebody sat down and
wrote things down.

---

### What they wrote on

Not a book. Books hadn't been invented.

They wrote on a scroll made from a reed that grew in the river, hammered flat and dried,
and the scroll they made was **twenty metres long**. Roll it out down a street and it
would pass a lot of front doors.

On it they put around **seven hundred** remedies. Seven hundred. Somebody had to collect
those. Somebody had to decide which ones were worth the space.

---

### The honey

Here is the thing they wrote down more than almost anything else, and here is why it is
interesting.

**Honey.** On wounds.

Now — they did not know about germs. Nobody knew about germs for another three thousand
years. Nobody had a microscope. Nobody could have told you *why* it worked.

But they could see. They could see that a wound with honey on it often did better than a
wound without. So they wrote it down, and the next person read it and did it too, and
that is what a scroll is *for*.

And here is the part that should stop you for a second: **hospitals still use honey on
wounds today.** Special medical honey, prepared carefully, not the jar in the kitchen. But
the same idea. Somebody noticed something true, wrote it on a reed, and it was still true
three and a half thousand years later.

> **This is important:** honey must never be given to a baby under one year old. It can
> make them very sick. The Egyptians had no way of knowing that. We do.

---

### The plant with two faces

There was another plant they grew, and this one has a face on each side.

Its seeds make an oil. Pressed out, cleaned up, that oil was used as a medicine, and a
version of it is still sold as a medicine now.

But the **seed itself** — the whole bean, before anybody presses anything — is one of the
most poisonous things that grows anywhere. Not a little bit poisonous. Genuinely deadly.

Same plant. The oil, and the poison, sitting in the same seed. The difference is entirely
in what people did to it afterwards.

Nobody in Kemet could explain the chemistry. They just knew the process, and they kept to
it, and passing that on correctly mattered more than almost anything else they wrote.

That is why this companion does not tell you how anything was made. Some knowledge is only
safe with the whole of it.

---

### The name on the scroll

One more thing, and it is worth knowing.

That scroll has a name. It is called the **Ebers Papyrus** — named for Georg Ebers, a
German man who bought it in 1873.

He did not write it. He was not born for another three thousand three hundred years after
it was written.

The people who *did* write it — the ones who collected the seven hundred remedies, who
noticed the honey, who worked out how to handle the dangerous seed — we do not have their
names. Nobody wrote those down, or if they did, that part did not survive.

So when you hear this scroll called by a buyer's name, you are hearing something true
about how old things get passed along and who ends up with their name on them. It is worth
noticing. It is worth asking about.

---

### The question to take with you

They watched. They wrote it down. They passed it on.

Some of what they wrote turned out to be right. Some of it turned out to be wrong. They
had no way to tell which was which — no tests, no way to check.

**How do you think they decided what was worth writing on a twenty-metre scroll?**

And the harder one:

**What are we writing down right now that someone in three thousand years will read, and
be able to tell straight away that we got wrong?**
