He picked the right tree
for completely the wrong reason.

In 1763 an English parson chose willow bark because willow grows in wet ground and fevers came from wet ground. The logic was worthless. The bark was not.

DOCUMENTED
Edward Stone gave willow bark to around fifty people with agues and wrote it up for the Royal Society in 1763.
TRADITION
He chose it by the doctrine of signatures — the belief that a plant growing where an illness comes from carries its answer.
MODERN SCIENCE
Willow bark holds salicin, and the line runs from there to aspirin — but willow bark is still not aspirin.
UNKNOWN
Whether the doses people actually took were ever near enough to matter. No dose records survive.

Willow bark is not aspirin.

This is the page that protects the reader, so it is placed before anything that could be mistaken for encouragement. The chemistry line from bark to drug is real and dated. It does not run backwards: the existence of a manufactured drug derived from a plant is not evidence about the plant as people actually used it.

DOCUMENTED
Acetylsalicylic acid irreversibly acetylates COX-1 in platelets. The salicylate route from willow does not act on platelets in the same way, so the two are not interchangeable.
DOCUMENTED
Salicin content varies widely between Salix species and with harvest conditions, so 'willow bark' is not one consistent material.
UNKNOWN
Whether any raw bark preparation reproduces results seen in studied standardised extracts. Not established.
THYLORA reads it this way

The interesting thing is not that the old use was vindicated. It is that it was vindicated sideways — the plant led to a drug that works differently from the plant. That gap is where most plant writing quietly cheats.
