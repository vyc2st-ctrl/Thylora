## HEADLINE

## SUPPORTING LINE

> In 1763 an English parson chose willow bark because willow grows in wet ground and fevers
> came from wet ground. The logic was worthless. The bark was not.

## QUICK FACT POINTS

Each point carries its register. The registers are the product — they appear on the image,
not just in the caption.

1. **DOCUMENTED** — Edward Stone gave willow bark to around fifty people with agues and wrote
   it up for the Royal Society in 1763.
2. **TRADITION** — He chose it by the doctrine of signatures: a plant that grows where the
   illness comes from was believed to carry the answer. That idea is folklore, not method.
3. **DOCUMENTED** — Willow bark holds salicin. Isolated in the 1820s, converted to salicylic
   acid in 1838, and the line runs from there to the acetylsalicylic acid Bayer sold as
   Aspirin from 1899.
4. **DOCUMENTED** — Aspirin is named after meadowsweet (*Spiraea*), not after willow.
5. **MODERN SCIENCE** — Willow bark is still not the same thing as aspirin. Aspirin
   permanently alters platelets; the salicylate route from willow does not work that way, and
   salicin content swings widely between willow species.
6. **UNKNOWN** — Nobody knows whether the doses people actually took across those centuries
   were anywhere near enough to matter. No dose records survive.
