## 2. WHY WILLOW SHOULD BE FIRST

Four reasons, in decreasing order of weight.

**It proves the format on the first release.** The shelf's product is not plant trivia — it
is the four-register separation: documented history, tradition, modern science, unknown.
Willow is the one candidate where all four registers are simultaneously rich *and*
independently checkable. The reader can verify the method on File 001 rather than being
asked to trust it, which is what a File 001 has to earn.

**It carries a real attribution dispute, so the shelf's standard is set at the start.**
The Hoffmann/Eichengrün question is a documented, unresolved dispute in the peer-reviewed
record about who deserves credit and why the mainstream account settled where it did.
Putting a contested attribution in File 001 — labelled CONTESTED, not resolved in either
direction — establishes at the outset that this shelf reports disputes rather than tidy
origin stories. Every later file inherits that standard instead of having to introduce it.

**It is the safest possible first release on claims.** The strong, citable evidence here is
about a *synthesised drug*, not about the raw herb. That lets File 001 be genuinely specific
and still make no health claim whatsoever about willow bark. The raw-herb evidence is in fact
weak and inconsistent — and saying so plainly is the most useful thing the file does. A first
release that demonstrates the shelf refusing an easy claim is worth more than one that has to
be hedged after the fact.

**It has the best single hook on the shelf.** Edward Stone chose willow in 1763 by the
doctrine of signatures — willow grows in damp ground, agues come from damp ground, so the
tree should hold the cure. The reasoning was wrong. The result was real. That is a question
the reader cannot put down, and it belongs to willow alone.

## 4. TRUTH CLASSES

### DOCUMENTED
- Ebers Papyrus (Egypt, c. 1550 BCE) contains remedies using willow. *(Caveat carried in the
  product: identification of plant names in the papyrus depends on translation and is not
  uniformly agreed.)*
- Dioscorides, *De Materia Medica* (c. 50–70 CE), records willow leaf and bark preparations.
- Edward Stone's letter to the Royal Society, read 1763, published in *Philosophical
  Transactions* — an account of willow bark given to around fifty people with agues.
  Stone states his reasoning from the doctrine of signatures in the letter itself.
- Salicin isolated and named — Buchner (1828), crystallised by Leroux (1829).
- Piria converts salicin to salicylic acid (1838).
- Gerhardt prepares acetylsalicylic acid in impure form (1853) and does not pursue it.
- Kolbe's synthesis route (1859) makes salicylic acid cheap at industrial scale.
- Bayer prepares stable acetylsalicylic acid (1897); marketed as Aspirin from 1899.
- The name *Aspirin* derives from acetyl + *Spiraea* (meadowsweet), **not** from willow.
- Vane establishes the prostaglandin-inhibition mechanism (1971); Nobel Prize 1982.
- Salicin is a prodrug: converted in the gut and liver to saligenin and then salicylic acid.
- Acetylsalicylic acid irreversibly acetylates COX-1 in platelets; salicylic acid derived
  from willow does not act on platelets in the same way. Willow bark is therefore **not**
  pharmacologically interchangeable with aspirin.
- Salicin content varies widely between *Salix* species and with harvest conditions.
- Documented salicylate risk signals exist, including salicylate sensitivity and asthma
  reactions, interaction with anticoagulants, gastrointestinal effects, and the association
  between salicylates and Reye's syndrome in children with viral illness.

### TRADITION / FOLK PRACTICE
- Chewing willow bark and drinking bark decoctions recur across European and North American
  folk records. Ethnobotanical records for Indigenous North American nations document willow
  use, but practices differ by nation and most surviving records were written by outside
  observers — the product says so rather than flattening them into one "traditional use".
- The doctrine of signatures — that a plant's habitat or appearance advertises its use —
  is the tradition that produced Stone's choice.

### INFERENCE / ANALYSIS *(THYLORA's reasoning, labelled as such)*
- Willow's persistence across unconnected regions is consistent with people observing a real
  effect, but tradition breadth is not evidence of efficacy and the file says so.
- Stone's result is best read as a correct outcome from incorrect reasoning — the file's
  organising argument, and explicitly an interpretation rather than a documented finding.
- The candidate ranking and the assignment of kola nut to File 002.

### CONTESTED *(documented dispute, unresolved — presented as a dispute)*
- Credit for the 1897 aspirin work. Bayer's account credits Felix Hoffmann. Arthur
  Eichengrün, a Bayer chemist who was Jewish and was imprisoned at Theresienstadt, wrote in
  1949 that he had directed the work and that the record was altered under Nazi-era
  authorship rules. Walter Sneader re-examined the case in the *BMJ* (2000) and argued
  Eichengrün's account fits the laboratory record better. Bayer disputes this. **The file
  presents both accounts and resolves neither.**

### UNKNOWN
- Which *Salix* species the Egyptian and Sumerian sources actually refer to.
- Whether historical preparations delivered salicylate exposure sufficient to produce the
  effects recorded — no dose data survives.
- Whether any raw bark preparation reproduces the results seen in studied standardised
  extracts. Not established.
- Long-term safety of willow bark extracts. Not established.
- Who actually directed the 1897 work — see CONTESTED.
- `BRAND-THYLORA-MARK`, `BRAND-FONT-FAMILY`, `BRAND-QR-DESTINATIONS` remain UNKNOWN /
  NOT_APPROVED and constrain this shelf exactly as they constrained Edition v2.

- Willow as File 001; kola nut as File 002.
