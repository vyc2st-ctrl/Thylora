ZONE 1 · LOCAL STORY CORE

A Bell Crossing girl was marked wrong on a sum she could do.

Denise Carter reports

ZONE 2 · CORRESPONDENT VOICE · STILL + VOICE
[VOICE — measured, unhurried. Room tone: a kitchen, evening.]

This looked like a maths problem. It wasn’t that simple.

I have known Maren Rowan a long time. So when she told me — over a cup of tea, not as an answer to a reporter’s question — that her granddaughter had been marked wrong on a sum she could do, I asked if I could see the sheet.

Talia Rowan is ten. She lives four streets from the mill road with Maren, who clerks the municipal records here. Last week Talia brought home six questions and got five right.

The sixth was about flour — how much a store room holds when some comes in and some goes out. She got it wrong. It went in the book as a maths mark.

I’ve read the sheet. The arithmetic on it is correct. What she missed was three words: every other day.

In Bell Crossing we say every other to mean the rest of them — every other child in the row, every other house on the street. On that sheet it meant every second day. Nothing on the page said which.

Read the way Talia read it, the flour goes up. Read the way the question meant it, the flour goes down. Same sums. Opposite answers. And the thing that decided it had no numbers in it at all.

Maren told me she nearly let it stand herself. Then she stopped and asked what the sentence meant instead of what the answer was. She has spent her working life reading other people’s records. She said it was the first time she had thought to read her granddaughter’s.

[END VOICE — approx. 55 seconds at reporting pace.]
