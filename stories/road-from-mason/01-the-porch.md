# Sixty Census House: The Road From Mason

## Chapter 1 · The Porch

*Draft 1 · Writers' Loft · Story Lead: Ray Bannister*
*Every fact in this chapter is in `lineage/PEETE-WRIGHT-LINEAGE.md`.*

---

The man came in off Mason Street a little after eight, and he did not sit down.

Most people sit. The Porch is built for it: two rockers, a glider, a cooler of
sweet tea that Odessa Harrow refills herself because she does not trust anybody
else to get the sugar right. Most people sit, and they hold a shoebox on their
knees, and they say *I don't know where to start.*

This man stood by the scanner with his phone out and said, "I know where to
start. I need you to know where to go."

Dess liked him immediately.

"Name," she said.

"Walter James Peete. My father. Thirty-six to ninety."

"Born where?"

"Tennessee. Mason, or Covington. Somewhere in Tipton County. He came up to
St. Louis. We were on North 19th Street." He said the house number the way people
say a house number they will never stop knowing.

Dess wrote *WALTER JAMES PEETE · 1936–1990* at the top of the case board in
black marker. Under it she drew two lines going down, one on each side, and
left them empty.

"Your mother?"

"Eddie Ree. Wright, before. From Mississippi. Sledge."

She wrote *EDDIE REE WRIGHT* on the right-hand line.

"Tell me one thing about the family," she said. "Not a date. A thing."

He laughed. It was the first time he'd sat down.

"After my father's funeral," he said, "everybody was at the house. My Aunt Lois,
she had polio when she was a girl, she walked with a lean. And my Uncle Teddy,
he was funny, he was always funny. They were going somewhere, all of them, out
the door, and he turns around and says, *Lois, get cho one-legged self out
here.*" He shook his head. "And she went. Laughing. Everybody laughing. At my
father's funeral."

Ray Bannister had come down from the Loft for coffee and stayed on the stairs.
He was writing on his hand.

"That's the first line of something," Ray said.

"It's the first line of *this*," said Dess. She tapped the right-hand side of
the board. "Lois. Your mother's sister?"

"Yes ma'am."

"Then she's on this side." Dess wrote *LOIS* under *EDDIE REE*, with a small
pencil mark beside it. Pencil, not marker. "Pencil means we believe you and we
haven't proved it yet. Marker means a record says so. Nothing on this board goes
from pencil to marker because somebody wants it to."

"Even me?"

"Especially you. It's your family. You'll want it too much."

---

Pip Okafor had the number by 8:40.

That is how the Desk does it: the Social Security file first, because a man
filled it out himself, or his mother did, and the parents' names are in it.
Pip read it out from the Reading Room doorway.

"Walter J. Peete. Born eighth of May, nineteen thirty-six. Mason, Tipton County,
Tennessee. Father, Willie R. Peete. Mother, Hattie Tate."

"Tate," the man said. "I didn't know Tate."

"There's more," Pip said. "Same father, same mother: a Willie Junior, born
twenty-eight. An Elmer, born thirty. A Wilbert, born thirty-three."

"Uncle Elmer. Uncle Wilbert." He said the names like he was setting them down
on a table. "They're right. Those are right."

Dess uncapped the black marker and wrote *WILLIE R. PEETE* and *HATTIE TATE*
above Walter's name, and the four brothers beside it. Then she stopped.

"Elmer's file says his mother is Hattie Whitley," Pip said. "Not Tate."

The room waited.

"Then we write both," Dess said, "and we go find her death certificate, and
the certificate tells us which one. We do not pick the one we like."

She wrote *HATTIE TATE (Whitley on one record)* and drew a box around the
parenthesis. A box on the board means *open question*. By the end of the week
there would be eleven boxes. By the end of the month, the Desk hoped, four.

---

**Earth fact.** The U.S. Social Security application (form SS-5) asks for the
applicant's father's full name and mother's maiden name. For many Black families
whose earlier records are thin, a parent's own SS-5 is the first document that
names the grandparents.

**THYLORA fact.** Sixty Census House was the Port District's cotton exchange.
The trading floor where prices were once called out is now the Reading Room,
and the old price board is the case board.

**The math.** Walter was born 8 May 1936. He died 7 September 1990. He lived
54 years, 3 months, 30 days.
