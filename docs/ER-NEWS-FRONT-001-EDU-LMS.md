### Item 5 — Opening column

> The paper said *twelve*. The paper said *miles*. The paper said *flour*. It also said
> *approximately*, *remaining*, and *at this rate*, and by the third of those the boy had stopped
> reading and started guessing.
>
> He got it wrong. The mark went in the book as a mark about arithmetic.
>
> It was not a mark about arithmetic. Nobody in the room had established that he could not
> divide. What had been established was that he could not get to the division.

### Item 6 — The equation block

**Formula and meaning appear together, always, on the same surface. They are never separated.**

> ### P_solve = L × M × S
>
> | Term | Name | What it asks |
> |---|---|---|
> | **L** | **LANGUAGE ACCESS** | Can the learner read and parse the words the problem is written in — including the connective words that carry the logic? |
> | **M** | **MATHEMATICAL OPERATION** | Can the learner carry out the arithmetic or procedure the problem requires, once it is known? |
> | **S** | **SITUATION MODEL** | Can the learner map the words onto a structure — who has what, what is changing, what is being asked for? |
>
> Each term is scored **0 or 1** for a single problem. `P_solve` is therefore **1 only when all
> three hold**. Any one at zero makes the product zero.

### Item 7 — The three-part test

> Three failures look identical on a score sheet and are not the same event.
>
> **L = 0 — the question did not arrive.** The words blocked access. The learner may or may not
> be able to do the mathematics; **this problem cannot tell you**.
>
> **S = 0 — the question arrived and did not resolve.** The learner read every word and could not
> assemble them into a situation. This is a modelling failure, not a reading failure and not an
> arithmetic failure.
>
> **M = 0 — the question arrived, resolved, and the procedure failed.** This is the only one of
> the three that is evidence about mathematics.

### Item 8 — What a zero does not prove

> **`P_solve = 0` names a blocked path. It does not name the cause.**
>
> When **L = 0**, `M` and `S` are **UNASSESSED** — not zero, not low, not proven. The problem
> never reached them. Recording them as failures manufactures a deficiency out of a measurement
> gap.
>
> The symmetric error is as bad: when **L = 0**, competence is not proven either. A learner who
> cannot read the question has not demonstrated that they *could* have answered it.
>
> **Do not infer either competence or deficiency without testing the relevant layer.**
> Test `M` with the same operation stated in numbers alone. Test `S` with the same situation
> stated in the learner's strongest language. Only then is there evidence.

### Item 10 — What would change the conclusion

> If the same learner, given the same operation stripped of prose, completes it — the original
> mark was never about mathematics, and every record derived from it is wrong.
>
> If the learner fails the stripped version too, `M = 0` is established, and the language question
> remains separately open rather than answered.

### Item 11 — Sidebar: one action each

> **For the teacher.** Before recording a wrong answer on a word problem, re-ask the operation
> with no prose. One minute. It changes what the mark means.
>
> **For the parent.** Ask your child to tell you what the question *wanted*, not what the answer
> was. If they cannot say what it wanted, the arithmetic was never the obstacle.

> edition its opening line — twelve miles, flour, a rate — is an EdereAriah story. The
> measurement problem it illustrates is an Earth one.
