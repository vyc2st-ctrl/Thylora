**Why the school's own status is unsettled in EP001 `[S]`:** the school is in the
middle of becoming something else. Its name is changing, its legal status is
undecided, its stationery is headed with a word the law has just abolished, and
its stock books are being re-ruled. **A record system in mid-transition is where
a book goes missing.** This is not coincidence in the design; it is the design.

### 2.2 · Fabric `[S]`

- Local red brick with stone dressings; steep slate roof; a small bell-cote with
  one bell, rung by hand at 8.55 and 1.25.
- **Windows sill-high at 4 ft** — deliberately above a child's eye line, as
  Victorian school building generally was.
- Deal floorboards, scrubbed. Dado painted dark green to shoulder height,
  distemper above.
- **Two coke stoves** with tall iron guards — one in the Main Room, one in Room III.
- Gas mantles survive in the corridor and cloakroom; **electric pendant lamps**
  in the classrooms `[S]`, from the Corve Valley Electricity & Gas Company
  `[E→S] S-20`.
- **Blackout frames still stacked behind the coke store** — removed, not yet
  disposed of. A precise November-1945 detail: the war ended six months ago and
  the fittings have not caught up.
- The **offices** (privies) are outside, across the yard. Cold, limewashed,
  emptied by the council `[E→S] S-18`.

| Husband | **Frank Harrowell**, 41, serving; **not yet demobilised** in November 1945 `[S]` |
| Children | None |

### 5.1 · Her documented spine `[E] S-07`

Edith Pryce was appointed to a school in 1928. She married in **1930** and was
required to resign, because **marriage bars were applied by local authorities**
and varied by locality `[E] S-07`. She taught unpaid, then as an uncertificated
supply teacher, then through the war as staff went to the services.

**Section 24 of the Education Act 1944 provides that no woman shall be
disqualified for employment as a teacher, or dismissed, by reason only of
marriage** `[E] S-07`. Her present post is the first permanent appointment she
has held in fifteen years.

### 5.2 · Why this matters to EP001 `[S]`

She has personally been **removed from a record and put back into one.** She
therefore keeps her stock book, her register and her marks with an exactness that
the head thinks excessive and that the episode proves is not. When the count
comes out at eleven, her first instinct is **not** to suspect a child — it is to
check her own arithmetic, out loud, in front of them. **She models the method the
children then use.**

### 5.3 · Manner

Dry, quick, undramatic. Uses children's surnames. Does not raise her voice.
Keeps a tin of chalk stubs she will not throw away. Will say *"Then we are
wrong somewhere, and we shall find out where"* — which is, functionally, the
adult translation of WYCK.

**Not `[S]`:** she is not a martinet, not a saint, not comic. She is a competent
professional in a tired building.

### 8.1 · The arithmetic exercise — exactly as set `[S]`

Chalked by Mrs Harrowell on the easel board at 09.31:

```
        Four dozen exercise books were received for this class.
        Thirty-six were given out.
        How many should remain?
```

- Four dozen = **48** received `[S]`
- **36** issued on Monday 19 November — **one to each child present.**
  Two of the 38 on roll were absent and were not issued (§6.2)
- 48 − 36 = **12 should remain**

The class does the sum. The answer is twelve.

**Then Donald Wakeley comes back from measles and needs a book.** Mrs Harrowell
unlocks the stock cupboard to issue one — and counts, on shelf 2, in front of
thirty-eight children:

> **Eleven.**

She counts again. Eleven. She says the class's own sum back to them and finds
no fault in it.

Bramble asks **why**.

WYCK arrives — at its quietest register — because a stated fact and an observed
fact have disagreed (`W-2`).

### 8.2 · The subtlety already inside the sum

The sum is **correct and complete as arithmetic** and **incomplete as a record**:
it silently assumes that "given out" and "on roll" are the same set, and they
are not — 38 on roll, 36 issued. **The class has just been shown, without being
told, that a true sum can still describe the world wrongly.** That is the
episode's thesis, planted in its first four minutes, in chalk.

## 9 · Uniforms and clothing at school `[S]`

**Sourcing note, held honestly:** this production has **not** sourced a
documented statement on uniform practice in English village primary schools in
1945. The following is therefore recorded as **simulation design**, and is
flagged for sourcing before any costume commitment (`U-07`).

| Element | Corvesley `[S]` |
|---|---|
| Uniform | **None.** Children wear ordinary clothes; there is no school cap, tie or badge |
| Boys | Short trousers to age 12–13; jersey or pullover; shirt; braces; long socks; hobnailed or stout boots; a coat or mackintosh; caps and knitted hats in winter |
| Girls | Frock or skirt and jumper; cardigan; pinafore for messy work; long socks; strong shoes; a coat; knitted hat and scarf in winter |
| Governing fact | **Clothing coupons were at their lowest in 1945–46** `[E] S-17`. Nothing is new. Everything is mended, handed down, let out, darned |
| Indoors | Coats and hats in the **cloakroom** on numbered pegs. **Peg numbers match desk numbers** `[S]` — a custody detail the children will use |
| Wet weather | The cloakroom smells of wet wool and coke all winter |
| **Bramble** | **Deep-red knitted winter cap with a soft pom** (C-1) and **selective deep-red scarf accents** (C-2), over preserved brown/earth clothing (C-3). Knitted by Ada Bramble from unravelled wool — **no coupons spent** `[E→S] S-17`. See `BIBLE-EP001.md` §3.3 |
